\chapter{Validation details}
\label{app:validation}

Chapter~\ref{ch:metrics}, \S\ref{sec:me-crossval} reported what the cross-validation against
independent implementations found. This appendix gives the procedures in enough detail to repeat
them, records the exact versions used, and documents the convention mismatches that had to be
resolved --- which are, in practice, the most reusable part of the exercise, because each one is a
trap that any reimplementation of these algorithms will hit.

\section{FilterPy: EKF, UKF and RTS smoother}

\subsection{Procedure}

\code{scripts/validate\_filterpy.py} re-implements \code{estkit::EcmModel} from scratch in NumPy.
The replica is not a wrapper: it re-derives $f$, $h$, $\mF$, $\mH$, $\mB$, $\mQ$ and $\mR$ from the
equations of Chapter~\ref{ch:ecm}, including the Arrhenius scaling, the coulombic efficiency, the
one-state hysteresis, the entropic OCV term and the piecewise-linear table interpolation with its
\code{floor}-and-clamp index arithmetic. It reads the parameters from \code{val\_params.csv} and the
table from \code{val\_ocv\_table.csv} so that the only shared quantity is data, never code.

The three estimators are then driven sample by sample over the exported dataset and compared
against the exported C++ trajectories with $\max_k\abs{\Delta\cdot}$ over all \num{20100} samples.

\subsection{Conventions that had to be matched}

\paragraph{Evaluation point of $\mQ$.} \code{Ekf::predict} computes $\xminus_k = f(\xplus_{k-1},\vu_{k-1})$
\emph{first} and then forms $\mQ(\xminus_k,\vu_{k-1})$, so the process-noise covariance --- which for
this model depends on the state through the hysteresis row of $\partial f/\partial i$ --- is
evaluated at the predicted state. FilterPy's \code{predict()} adds a pre-set \code{self.Q}. The
driver therefore evaluates the predicted mean itself, sets \code{ekf.Q = m.Q(xpred, i, T)}, and only
then calls \code{predict}.

\paragraph{Input timing.} The $\vu_{k-1}$/$\vu_k$ convention of \S\ref{sec:me-timing-convention} is
reproduced exactly: \code{predict} is skipped at $k=0$ and otherwise called with the previous
sample's measured current and temperature, while the measurement update uses the current sample's.

\paragraph{Symmetrisation and the state constraint.} $\mP\gets\frac12(\mP+\mP^\T)$ after both
updates, and the clamp $\soc\in[-0.05,1.05]$, $h\in[-1,1]$ after the measurement update.

\paragraph{Nonlinear propagation in FilterPy's EKF.} \code{filterpy.kalman.ExtendedKalmanFilter}
propagates linearly by default; the driver subclasses it and overrides \code{predict\_x} so that the
nonlinear $f$ is used, with \code{self.F} set to the analytic Jacobian.

\subsection{The two unscented variants}

The \code{estkit} UKF supports two measurement updates, selected by
\code{opts.redraw\_sigma\_points}:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item \code{true} (default, used everywhere in the benchmark): the sigma points are \emph{redrawn}
from $(\xminus_k,\Pminus_k)$ before the measurement update, so that the additive $\mQ$ added during
the time update is reflected in the spread of the points and therefore in $\mP_{yy}$. This is
Särkkä~\cite[Alg.~5.14]{sarkka2013} and Plett~\cite{plett2006spkf1}.
\item \code{false} (used only for this comparison): the propagated points $\mathcal{X}^f$ are
\emph{reused}, which is Wan and van der Merwe's original formulation~\cite[Alg.~3.1]{wan2000ukf}
and what FilterPy implements.
\end{itemize}

These are not algebraically equivalent --- reuse under-represents $\mQ$ in $\mP_{yy}$ and is
therefore mildly over-confident --- so the comparison is run with
\code{f.opts.redraw\_sigma\_points = false} (see the \code{CellEstimator} construction in
\code{tools/export\_validation.cpp}). Any report of a ``UKF'' result that does not state which
variant it used is under-specified.

\paragraph{The $k=0$ sigma points.} FilterPy's \code{UnscentedKalmanFilter.update()} reads
\code{self.sigmas\_f}, which is an array of zeros until \code{predict()} has been called once. At
$k=0$ no time update is performed, so the driver explicitly seeds
\code{ukf.sigmas\_f = pts.sigma\_points(x0, P0)} --- which is what \code{estkit} does internally
(\code{have\_propagated\_ == false} makes it draw from the current $(\vx,\mP)$). Without this the
first update runs on zeroed sigma points and the two trajectories diverge from the very first
sample. Sigma-point parameters are $\alpha = 0.1$, $\beta = 2$, $\kappa = 0$ on both sides.

\paragraph{The predicted measurement.} Both sides compute
$\hat y_k = \sum_s w^m_s\, h(\mathcal{X}^f_s,\vu_k)$, the sigma-weighted mean, rather than
$h(\xminus_k,\vu_k)$. The two differ by the curvature of $h$ and the difference is not negligible
on the OCV plateau.

\subsection{The RTS smoother and the affine change of variables}

\code{filterpy.kalman.rts\_smoother(Xs, Ps, Fs, Qs)} implements the strictly linear
Rauch--Tung--Striebel recursion~\cite{rauch1965rts}. It reconstructs the predicted mean internally as
$\mF_k\xplus_k$ and therefore assumes a system with no input term, whereas the battery model has
one. The model is, however, \emph{affine} in the state --- $\mF_k$ does not depend on $\vx$ --- so
the input can be removed exactly. With
\begin{equation}
\vx_{k+1} = \mF_k\vx_k + \bm{b}_k + \vw_k,\qquad \bm{b}_k = f(\bm 0,\vu_k),
\qquad \bm{c}_0 = \bm 0,\quad \bm{c}_{k+1} = \mF_k\bm{c}_k + \bm{b}_k,
\label{eq:av-affine}
\end{equation}
the shifted state $\tilde{\vx}_k = \vx_k - \bm{c}_k$ satisfies $\tilde{\vx}_{k+1} = \mF_k\tilde{\vx}_k
+ \vw_k$ with the same $\mF_k$ and $\mQ_k$, and all covariances are unchanged because the shift is
deterministic. The driver therefore runs the forward EKF storing $(\xplus_k,\Pplus_k,\mF_k,\mQ_k)$,
subtracts $\bm{c}_k$, calls \code{rts\_smoother}, and adds $\bm{c}_k$ back.

Two indexing details matter. \code{rts\_smoother} expects $\mF_k,\mQ_k$ to be the matrices that
carry the state from $k$ to $k+1$, whereas the forward loop naturally records the ones used to
arrive \emph{at} $k$; the arrays are therefore shifted by one, with the last entry repeated. And the
particular solution \eqref{eq:av-affine} must be accumulated with the shifted $\mF_k$, not the
recorded one.

The residual disagreement is $7.3\times10^{-9}$ in the smoothed SOC, against $10^{-15}$ for the
forward EKF. Two mechanisms explain the difference and neither is a defect. The backward pass forms
$\hat{\vx}^s_k = \tilde{\vx}^+_k + \mC_k(\tilde{\vx}^s_{k+1} - \tilde{\vx}^-_{k+1}) + \bm{c}_k$, a
difference of nearly equal $O(1)$ quantities once $\bm{c}_k$ has grown to $O(1)$, so there is genuine
cancellation; and the forward pass applies the state clamp, which is a nonlinearity that the change
of variables does not commute with, so the two implementations see slightly different stored
posteriors whenever the clamp is active. Both effects are four orders of magnitude below the
\num{1e-4} scale at which any SOC result is quoted.

\section{PyBaMM: the single-particle plant}

\subsection{Procedure}

\code{scripts/validate\_pybamm.py} builds the PyBaMM model \code{lithium\_ion.SPM()} with the
parameter set \code{ParameterValues('Chen2020')}~\cite{chen2020parameterization}, sets the current to
$C\times\SI{5}{\ampere}$, fixes ambient and initial temperature at \SI{298.15}{\kelvin}, and sets
\code{Contact resistance [Ohm]} to zero where the installed version exposes it. The C++ side is the
same model at its published-parameter settings: \code{SpmPlant<20>} with
\code{enable\_thermal = false}, \code{R\_ohm\_ref = 0}, \code{r\_scale = k\_scale = 1}, discharged at
constant current from $\soc=1$ until the terminal voltage falls below \SI{2.5}{\volt}, recorded
every \SI{1}{\second}.

Both voltage traces are interpolated onto a common \SI{1}{\second} grid spanning the shorter of the
two, and the RMSE and maximum absolute difference are reported, together with the time at which each
reaches cut-off. Three C-rates are used: \SIlist{0.5;1.0;2.0}{} C.

\subsection{Interpretation}

The results (Chapter~\ref{ch:metrics}) are \SIrange{0.64}{3.17}{\milli\volt} RMSE with maximum
differences of \SIrange{13}{46}{\milli\volt}, and identical times to cut-off --- \SIlist{7230;3567;1735}{\second}
--- at all three rates. The agreement in cut-off time is the stronger result: it says the two
implementations share the same capacity, the same stoichiometry mapping
($x_{100}=0.9014$, $y_{100}=0.2700$, $\Delta x = 0.85798$, $\Delta y = 0.57259$) and the same
lithium balance, because a discrepancy in any of those would shift the knee.

The maximum differences all occur in the last few seconds before cut-off, where
$\abs{\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}t}$ exceeds \SI{50}{\milli\volt\per\second} and a sub-second timing
difference becomes a large voltage difference. Away from the knee the difference is below
\SI{5}{\milli\volt} everywhere. The residual is discretisation, not physics: \code{estkit} uses 20
finite-volume shells per particle, backward Euler at $\dt=\SI{0.1}{\second}$, and a linear
reconstruction of the surface concentration from the flux boundary condition, while PyBaMM uses its
default mesh and an adaptive DAE solver~\cite{andersson2019casadi}. The error growing with C-rate is
exactly what a fixed-mesh solid-diffusion discretisation should do, because the concentration
gradient inside the particle steepens.

\paragraph{What this does not show.} Agreement between two single-particle models is not evidence
that a single-particle model describes a cell. The SPM neglects electrolyte transport entirely, and
its error against a full Doyle--Fuller--Newman model grows with C-rate and with electrode thickness.
The plant is used here because it is a \emph{different model class} from the estimators' 2-RC
circuit, which is what makes the \code{spm} columns of the results tables a test of structural
model error; it is not used as a claim of physical fidelity.

\section{The \texttt{ahrs} library: Madgwick and Mahony}

\subsection{Procedure}

\code{scripts/validate\_ahrs.py} loads \code{val\_ahrs.csv} and runs
\code{ahrs.filters.Madgwick} (\code{frequency=100}, \code{beta=0.1}) and
\code{ahrs.filters.Mahony} (\code{frequency=100}, \code{k\_P=1.0}, \code{k\_I=0.1}) in their \code{updateMARG} (nine-axis)
mode, initialised from the true quaternion of the first sample. The \code{estkit} filters were
configured identically in \code{export\_validation.cpp} --- $\beta = 0.1$, $\zeta = 0$;
$k_P = 1$, $k_I = 0.1$ --- with \code{opts.gate = false} on both, so that neither implementation
applies the accelerometer-magnitude gating that the benchmark variants use, and
\code{opts.mag\_ref\_measured = true} on the Mahony so that both derive the magnetic reference the
same way.

The comparison metric is the angular distance between the two implementations' quaternions,
$2\arccos\abs{\langle q_a, q_b\rangle}$, which is sign-invariant and therefore immune to the double
cover of $SO(3)$.

\subsection{Convention mismatch 1: specific force versus gravity}

\code{estkit} stores the accelerometer channel as \emph{specific force},
$f = R^\T(a_{\text{nav}} - g_{\text{nav}})$, which at rest in the FRD body frame reads
$[0,0,-g]^\T$. The \code{ahrs} filters expect an accelerometer that reads $+g$ along body $z$ at
rest. The driver negates the channel before passing it to \code{ahrs}:
\code{acc = -d[:, [ax, ay, az]]}.

This is worth stating loudly because a sign error here does not produce an obvious failure: the
filter converges smoothly and confidently to an attitude that is upside down, which looks like a
working filter until the Euler angles are inspected.

\subsection{Convention mismatch 2: \texttt{ahrs}'s Mahony is hard-coded ENU}

\code{ahrs.filters.Madgwick} constructs its magnetic reference from the measurement, so it is
frame-agnostic. \code{ahrs.filters.Mahony} does not: it builds
$b = [\,0,\ \sqrt{h_x^2+h_y^2},\ h_z\,]$ --- magnetic north along the $+y$ axis --- which is an
East--North--Up navigation frame, incompatible with the NED/FRD dataset.

The two frame pairs are related by the proper rotation
\begin{equation}
\mP = \begin{bmatrix} 0&1&0\\ 1&0&0\\ 0&0&-1\end{bmatrix},
\qquad \det\mP = +1,
\qquad q_P = \Bigl[\,0,\ \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\ \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\ 0\,\Bigr],
\label{eq:av-enu}
\end{equation}
a \SI{180}{\degree} rotation about the axis $(1,1,0)/\sqrt2$, which swaps the first two axes and
inverts the third. The same matrix maps FRD to FLU, so it transforms body-frame and navigation-frame
vectors alike:
\begin{equation}
v_{\text{ENU}} = \mP\, v_{\text{NED}},
\qquad v_{\text{FLU}} = \mP\, v_{\text{FRD}},
\qquad q_{\text{ENU}} = q_P \otimes q_{\text{NED}} \otimes q_P^{-1}.
\label{eq:av-enuq}
\end{equation}
The driver rotates all three sensor vectors and the initial quaternion into the ENU/FLU pair, runs
\code{ahrs}, and rotates each output quaternion back with $q_P^{-1}\otimes\cdot\otimes q_P$. Note
that in the ENU/FLU pair the specific force is already what \code{ahrs} expects, so the negation of
the previous subsection is undone again for the Mahony path (\code{acc\_e = -acc @ P.T} in the
script, where \code{acc} has already been negated once).

Checking that $\det\mP = +1$ is not pedantry: the naive ``swap $x$ and $y$'' matrix without the sign
flip on $z$ is a reflection, and quaternions cannot represent it. A reflection substituted here
would silently mirror the attitude.

\subsection{Convention mismatch 3: quaternion propagation}

\code{estkit} propagates with the exact exponential map,
$\hat q_k = \hat q_{k-1}\otimes\exp(\bm\omega\dt)$ where $\exp(\bm\rho) = [\cos\frac{\norm{\bm\rho}}{2},\
\frac{\bm\rho}{\norm{\bm\rho}}\sin\frac{\norm{\bm\rho}}{2}]$; \code{ahrs} takes a first-order Euler
step of $\dot q = \frac12 q\otimes[0,\bm\omega]$ followed by renormalisation. The per-step
discrepancy is $O\bigl((\norm{\bm\omega}\dt)^2\bigr)$; at the dataset's peak rate of
\SI{14.3}{\degree\per\second} and $\dt = \SI{0.01}{\second}$ that is about
\SI{5e-5}{\degree} per step, which the filters' own feedback keeps bounded. This is the mechanism
behind the observed \SI{0.0014}{\degree} peak difference, and it is irreducible without changing one
implementation or the other.

\subsection{Why the attitude errors are large}

Both implementations report attitude RMS errors against the truth of \SI{17.604}{\degree}
(Madgwick) and \SI{22.639}{\degree} (Mahony) --- identical to three decimals. These are not
implementation defects and they are not a criticism of the published algorithms. They are the
coordinated turns of the dataset (Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets}, \S\ref{sec:ds-imu}): an ungated
gravity-referenced filter reads the \SI{0.47}{}g sustained lateral specific force of a
\SI{25}{\degree} banked turn as tilt and rolls its estimate into it, and these two configurations
have gating disabled precisely so that the two implementations compute the same thing.

Reproducing a \emph{large} error with independent code is a stronger validation than reproducing a
small one: it confirms that the dataset, the frame conventions, the sensor model and the algorithm
all agree, since any mismatch in any of them would have produced two different large errors rather
than the same one. The gated variants registered in the benchmark
(\code{Madgwick-Gated}, \code{Mahony-Gated}) do substantially better, and the difference between the
gated and ungated rows in the attitude results is attributable entirely to the gate.

\section{A note on the bibliography}

The reference lists in this report were compiled by the authors from the primary literature and from
the reference headers of the source files they document. Volume and page numbers, and in particular
any DOIs, should be treated as \emph{transcribed from memory} rather than verified against the
publisher record, and should be checked before this bibliography is reused in a formal
publication. Where a bibliographic detail was uncertain it was recorded as given in the
implementation's own reference header, so that the report and the code at least agree with each
other; two entries known to warrant checking are the doubling-algorithm
references~\cite{anderson1978doubling,chu2004doubling}, whose venue and pagination the authors were
unable to confirm independently. The substantive content of every citation --- which algorithm,
which author, which year --- is reliable; the metadata is not.
